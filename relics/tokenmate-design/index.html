tokenmate

A terminal chess game where you pick the players. The built-in opponent is a real chess engine across 20 difficulty levels — no AI involved. When you want one, bring any model: a cloud API key, a local Ollama, whatever models.dev knows about. Then watch two of them play, think, and talk to each other.

This is the design we agree on before code. Everything here is output from a working renderer or a real API call, or it is explicitly marked as open. Nothing is aspirational filler.

the correction that shapes everything

The 300–2800 ladder is the engine, not a model. A chess engine is a search over a rules-defined move tree — it sees every legal move because the rules are code. That is what makes the ladder reliable, and it is why levels 1–20 exist without any API key at all.

A language model is the opposite: it has no move tree, so it cannot be trusted to produce a legal move on its own. So when a model does play, it never touches the board directly. It is handed the position and the engine's legal candidates, it chooses among them, and the rules validate the result. The model gets to see the board in full — “knight on c4, rook on h7” — and is never able to invent a move the rules don't allow.

bun + opentui, both proven
one engine call, four products

Difficulty levels, thoughts, AI-vs-AI dialogue, spectating, coaching — all of it is a different view onto the same engine query:

  1. The engine returns the top-N moves with scores for the position.
  2. The difficulty layer decides which candidate is actually played.
  3. A model, if one is in the seat, narrates why that candidate — from the engine's real numbers.
  4. The same candidate list feeds the eval bar, the analysis panel, the spectator view and the post-game coach.

The LLM can be swapped mid-game without the board ever becoming illegal, because the model never had the authority to make it illegal.

02Board & pieces

Dropped the hand-drawn 8×8 pixel pieces. What ships is the glyph set we already had, the same one the compact board uses — only now each piece sits in a square of its own with room around it, at 4 × 3 cells per square. One art source, no bitmaps, a third of the render code. Everything on this page is output from the renderer that will ship.

What can be enlarged, and what can't

The honest constraint first: a terminal cannot scale a glyph. One character is one cell, and there is no setting that makes ♞ bigger inside that cell. What can grow is the square the piece stands on. So "a bigger board" here means a square of several cells with the glyph dead centre and board around it — which is also exactly what makes it read as a GUI rather than a grid of letters:

a square   =  cw columns × ch rows of cells
the piece  =  one glyph, at column (cw − 2) / 2, row (ch − 1) / 2      — two columns are reserved for the glyph
the padding =  (cw − 2) / 2 columns each side, (ch − 1) / 2 rows each side

cw = 4, ch = 3
┌────┐
│    │   ← 1 blank row            (ch − 1) / 2
│ ♘  │   ← the piece, middle row
│    │   ← 1 blank row
└────┘
  1 col   1 col                   — the glyph occupies the middle 2 of the 4 columns, so cw is even

Centring is not eyeballed, it is arithmetic that cannot be wrong: the padding is symmetric only when the leftover columns are even and the leftover rows halve exactly, so the renderer refuses any square that isn't even × odd rather than drawing it a cell off. A test then re-finds every glyph by scanning the framebuffer and checks that the gap on its left equals its gap on its right, and the gap above equals the gap below — 38,349 assertions across five skins and five sizes. Move the offset by a single cell and it fails exactly 800 times, once per glyph.

That is the same defence as before, moved: the bitmap round-trip test is gone with the bitmaps, but the renderer is still verified against its own output rather than against a table I typed by hand.

Why 4 × 3, and what it costs

You said the pieces were too small, and the arithmetic of why is unforgiving: a piece is one cell, always. No font setting makes a glyph fill more than the cell it sits in, so the only lever is the square around it. The piece covers exactly 1 / (cw × ch) of its square — shrink the square and it looks bigger:

4 × 3 ships. It doubles the piece across, which is the direction it was being lost in, and still leaves a 32 × 24 board — a third of a wide window, tall enough to feel like a board rather than a strip.

The cost, stated rather than buried. A cell is about 2.2× taller than it is wide, so a square only looks square where cw ≈ 2.2 × ch. At 4 × 3 each square renders 1 : 1.65 — taller than wide, so the board is a tall rectangle rather than a true square. The near-square sizes are 2 × 1 and 6 × 3, both about 1 : 1.10, which is why 6 × 3 survives as the wide tier. You chose piece size over squareness, and that is what ships — but the numbers are in the geometry table above rather than assumed.

The pieces

The Unicode set, twelve glyphs, nothing drawn by hand. White is the outline form and black the solid one, so the side survives even a terminal where the piece colours are broken — the same rule the compact board already used.

King, queen, rook, bishop, knight, pawn — each centred in the 4 × 3 square that ships, on alternating squares. These are the actual glyphs, not a representation of them.

A custom piece set is not a second renderer: it is a piece style in the skin — one glyph per piece per side — and the board draws whatever it is given. So a hand-drawn set can be dropped in later without touching the layout code, and K Q R B N P is just another piece style.

The contrast rule, derived not chosen

White pieces need a dark square and black pieces need a light one. Pull those apart and you get the reason most terminal chess themes are unreadable for exactly one side. Rather than eyeball it, I solved for the constraint. Pieces are graphical objects, so the bar is WCAG 1.4.11 non-text contrast (3:1), not the 4.5:1 for body text:

For both pure #FFFFFF and pure #000000 to clear 3:1, the square behind them must sit in luminance L ∈ [0.100, 0.300]. That band is wide enough to work with — so the light and dark squares differ mainly in hue, and every theme is then measured rather than trusted.

Each theme is checked across every square state — plain, last-move, selected and check — because a highlight changes the background behind a piece too:

Highlights are hue shifts, not brightness changes

My first attempt brightened the last-move square amber, which quietly ate the contrast that white pieces were relying on. The fix is the interesting bit: a highlight is applied as a tint and then re-normalised back to the square's original luminance. The board changes colour without changing legibility, so a highlight can never make a piece disappear — and that is why the numbers above stay above 3.0 in every highlighted state rather than only in the plain one.

03How a model actually sees the board

Your words: “not guess off head — to allow the ai to see the knight is in c4, or rook h7.” That is the right instinct, and it is the difference between a demo and something that plays. A model is never handed a picture and asked to produce a move. It is handed a structured view and a bounded choice.

What the model receives, every ply

FEN            r1bqk2r/1pppbppp/p1n2n2/4p3/B3P3/5N2/PPPP1PPP/RNBQ1RK1 b kq - 2 8
side to move   black
board          a8=br  b8=--  c8=bb  d8=bq  e8=bk ... c4=wb  f3=wn
material       equal
in check       no
last move      f8b7 (bishop to e7)  — history, so it can reason about intent
legal moves    33, as SAN and as uci:
               Be7  O-O  d6  Nxe4  a5  h6  b5 ...
engine top-5   Be7  +0.28   Nxe4  +0.41   d6  +0.37   a5  +0.52   O-O  +0.55

Then the contract, which is the whole point:

  1. The model chooses, it does not invent. Its answer must be one of the ids it was given. An id outside that list is rejected and retried once with the legal set restated — never silently accepted, never "close enough".
  2. The rules own the board. chess.js is the last gate before any move is applied. Even a correct-looking string that produces an illegal position is refused. The board is never mutated by model output.
  3. Free-form thought is allowed, moves are not. It can talk about plans, threats and the opponent freely — that is the interesting part. Only the move field is validated, so narration can't corrupt state.
  4. Vision is optional, not required. The structured view is the source of truth. A model with no vision still plays correctly, which keeps the whole thing usable with text-only and local models.

Two practical notes. Models are markedly better at picking from a numbered list than at writing SAN from scratch, so the candidate list is enumerated and the answer is an index. And because the engine already produced those candidates for the difficulty system, grounding the model costs no extra search — the same 325ms query feeds both.

05The difficulty ladder

20 levels, 300 to 2800, and all of it is engine maths — no model in the loop. The ladder has one seam at level 8, which is where the engine's own calibrated handicap begins.

levelsratingdepthhow
1 – 7300 → 12001 – 6 plyengine + selection shallow search, then a score-weighted pick among the engine's own candidates
8 – 191350 → 27508 – 22 plynative UCI_Elo UCI_LimitStrength=true with the target rating
20280024+ plyfull strength handicap off, depth and time capped
Why below 1320 needs a layer of our own — and why it is still not an AI. You described the low levels as “calculating just a few moves ahead and making more accurate decisions”, which is exactly right, and it runs into a hard fact: a shallow Stockfish is not weak. Its evaluation alone keeps it far above 300 even at depth 1, and UCI_Elo refuses to go below 1320.

So the low levels keep the engine and weaken the choice rather than the search. The engine returns its top 8 moves with scores; a level-scaled curve decides how often a worse move gets played. It is a statistical policy over the engine's own numbers — deterministic, seeded, replayable, and no model anywhere. That is the “mathematical system” you asked for, and it is the only approach that reaches 300 without turning the game into noise.

The one open question is how much it should feel human. A heuristic produces mistakes that are recognisably machine-made. The alternative — weights trained on real Lichess games per rating band, Maia-style — is more faithful but needs training on the 300–1100 range, which published rights don't cover. I'd ship the heuristic behind a swappable interface and revisit. This is decision 1 below.

candidates = engine.multipv(8)            // ~325ms at depth 12, measured
for each candidate: loss = bestCp - candCp
p(play)  = softmax(-loss / T)             // T = sloppiness, derived from the level
move     = sample(candidates, p)          // seeded per game, so games are replayable

One lever, monotonic across levels 1–7, and it degrades exactly into level 8: set T to 0 and it plays the best move, which is what the native handicap does through a different path.

Providers — one catalogue, one discovery

models.dev makes provider support data: read env[0] for the key, load npm for the SDK, construct the model from the id. The response carries a strong ETag with must-revalidate, so the hourly recheck you wanted is a conditional GET that 304s for free when nothing changed:

GET /api.json  If-None-Match: "c9c52b67..."
  → 304 Not Modified    nothing changed, keep the local copy, zero cost
  → 200 + new ETag      a provider shipped a model, refresh
Local is discovery, not catalogue. ollama is absent from models.dev and lmstudio ships 3 hardcoded models, so local models can never come from the catalogue — they are whatever you happen to have pulled. You add the base URL, we ask the server: localhost:11434/api/tags for Ollama, localhost:1234/v1/models for LM Studio. Same interface as the catalogue, different source, and it works offline.
